Understand the connection

From a battery to a magnetic field.

An electromagnet is a magnet produced by electric current. Unlike a permanent magnet, you can switch its main field on and off.

01 / THE PUSH

Voltage drives current.

Voltage is energy per unit charge. With resistance held constant, more voltage produces more current: I = V ÷ R. Four 1.5 V cells connected positive to negative provide 6.0 V.

02 / THE FIELD

Moving charge makes magnetism.

Current in a wire creates a magnetic field around it. Wrap the wire into a coil and the fields combine. An iron core becomes magnetized and strengthens the field.

03 / THE BOOST

More turns can add strength.

At the same current and coil length, 50 turns produce twice the field of 25 turns in this model. Switch off the current and the main field disappears; real iron can retain some magnetism.

Beyond the workbench

Magnetism, everywhere.

Speakers use a changing current in a coil to move a cone. Electric motors use forces on current-carrying coils to turn a shaft. Relays use electromagnets to move a switch.

How does an electromagnet differ from a permanent magnet?

An electromagnet's main field depends on current. A permanent magnet stays magnetized without a power source. Strong heating or an opposing field can reduce permanent magnetism by disrupting domain alignment.

Would more voltage always be better?

Higher voltage can increase current and heating. At fixed resistance, power is V²/R, so doubling voltage quadruples heating power. Real cores can also saturate. This model omits heating and saturation.
