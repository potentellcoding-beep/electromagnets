Understand the connection

From a battery to a magnetic field.

An electromagnet is a magnet produced by electric current. Unlike a permanent magnet, you can switch its main field on and off.

01 / THE PUSH

Voltage drives current.

Voltage is energy per unit charge. For the same wire and battery model, more voltage produces more current: I = V ÷ R. Four 1.5 V cells connected positive to negative provide 6.0 V.

02 / THE FIELD

Moving charge makes magnetism.

Current in a wire creates a magnetic field around it. Wrap the wire into a coil and the fields combine. An iron core becomes magnetized and strengthens the field.

03 / THE BOOST

More turns can add strength.

At the same current and coil length, doubling the wraps doubles the field. Here, extra wire also adds resistance, so 50 wraps give about 1.67 times the field of 25 at the same voltage. Switch off the current and the clips drop.

Keep it a fair test.

You change: voltage in one series; wire turns in the next.
You measure: number of paperclips lifted.
You hold constant: core, coil length, paperclip mass, and pickup contact. Extra wraps add resistance.
Baseline: an open circuit, with zero current.

Change one variable at a time. Use the same wraps to compare voltages, then hold voltage constant to compare wraps. An open circuit is a useful zero-current comparison.

What the model means

Plausible counts. Clear assumptions.

These are estimates for one virtual setup, not predictions for every real nail. Small changes in contact or wire can change how many clips a real electromagnet lifts.

Our virtual setup

The four D cells stay in the holder. A fixed common wire and a movable wire select 1, 2, 3, or 4 cells in series. The schematic is inspired by the supplied holder photo; terminal positions are labeled for learning and are not a wiring guide for an unidentified physical holder.

30 identical small steel paperclips, 0.5 g each; a 20 mm² iron nail tip; an effective magnetic gap of 0.8 mm; fixed core and coil geometry. Total resistance is 2 Ω for battery/leads plus 0.02 Ω per wrap. Contact efficiency is modeled as 65%.

Wraps are drawn schematically. The pickup view shows a compact bundle against the nail tip, not a chain with a different magnetic gap at every clip.

How clips are counted

Current I = V/R. Estimated field B = μ₀NI/g, capped at 1.2 T. Estimated usable lift F = 0.65 × B²A/(2μ₀). Count = floor(F ÷ weight of one clip), limited to the 30 clips in the tray.

The effective gap and efficiency summarize leakage and imperfect contact. They are modeling assumptions, not experimentally calibrated values. Heating, battery drain, detailed magnetic geometry and residual magnetism are omitted.

Beyond the workbench

Magnetism, everywhere.

Speakers use a changing current in a coil to move a cone. Electric motors use forces on current-carrying coils to turn a shaft. Relays use electromagnets to move a switch.

How does an electromagnet differ from a permanent magnet?

An electromagnet's main field depends on current. A permanent magnet stays magnetized without a power source. Strong heating or an opposing field can reduce permanent magnetism by disrupting domain alignment.

Would more voltage always be better?

Higher voltage increases current and heating. At fixed resistance, power is V²/R, so doubling voltage quadruples heating power. Real cores can saturate. This virtual model does not heat up; real coils and batteries can. Follow a teacher-approved setup for a physical experiment.

What would your “magnetic touch” attract?

Imagine the story from the slides: you can attract iron and many steels, but not everything metal. Aluminum and copper do not stick like iron. What problem would you solve with your magnetic touch?
